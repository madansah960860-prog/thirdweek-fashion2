Method

The plate tells you what the model hides

By Marisol Okonkwo-Reyes · August 21, 2026 · 9 minute read

A styled photograph of a coat on a person tells you how that coat sits on that person. That is genuinely useful if you happen to share their proportions. For everyone else it is decoration, and worse than decoration, because it conceals the exact information you need before spending real money on a garment that exists in one size only.

We photograph every garment flat, on a plate, under raking light. A copy stand holds the camera at a fixed height above a matte black table. One lamp sits low and off to the side. The garment lies there, still, and the lens looks straight down at it. There is no body in the frame and there is nothing to be styled.

The decision was not aesthetic. It was practical, and it came in the second month of the business, when a customer returned a coat that had looked right in a photograph and measured wrong in her hallway.

What a flat plate does that a body cannot

A garment worn is a garment in motion, and motion is flattery. A sleeve pushed back to the forearm hides a fade line at the cuff. A shoulder turned three degrees toward the lens takes two inches off a boxy chest. A hand in a pocket hides the pocket. None of this requires anyone to be dishonest. It is what happens when cloth is draped on a moving frame and photographed from the front.

On the plate nothing moves and nothing is tucked. The whole front panel lies in one plane, every part of it within a quarter inch of every other part, and the lens looks at all of it from the same distance. A steel rule sits in the frame on the scale pass, so the scale is not a claim, it is visible. That one property is why our measurements and our photographs agree with each other.

The second advantage is the light. A lit portrait needs decisions: where the key goes, how soft the shadow is, whether to fill. Every one of those decisions is an opportunity to flatter. Our key lamp is bolted at a fixed height and a fixed angle, low and raking across the plate, and it does not move between garments. It cannot be adjusted to soften a stain or improve a fade line. Raking light is the least forgiving light there is, and that is precisely why we use it.

The third is resolution. We work at 600 pixels per inch across the plate, which sounds like a technical footnote until you open a file. At that density you can count the stitches in a knit and read the weave of a cotton duck. Somebody considering a Shetland can look at the gauge themselves rather than taking our word for seven stitches to the inch. Somebody worried about a repair can look at the darn at a scale a lifestyle photograph would never give them.

The question that decides most secondhand purchases is not what does this look like. It is what is wrong with it. A plate answers that question before it is asked.

The process, start to finish

A garment reaches the plate already cleaned, repaired and measured. The measurements go onto a paper record card in pen first, which sounds needlessly slow and turns out to matter: writing a number down forces a second look at the tape, and we have caught more transcription errors that way than we would like to admit.

The plate gives us roughly eleven by seventeen inches of usable surface under the lamp before the falloff at the edge becomes visible. No adult garment fits in that in one frame, so the first decision for every piece is how to fold it. That was the part of the method that took longest to work out.

For outerwear we lay the front panel across the plate with the collar at one edge and the closure running down the centre. The sleeves fold back underneath. One frame then shows the collar, the placket, the chest and the pocket placement continuously, with no join in the file.

For knitwear we fold at the natural break of the body and photograph the folded piece rather than pulling it flat. A sweater stretched out on a table reads a full size larger than it wears, because knit gives under even light pressure. Folded, it records at rest. This is the commonest mistake in flat-lay photography and it produces listings where the measurement and the picture disagree.

For belts and small leather goods we coil the strap so the full length sits inside the lit area, then measure separately on a steel rule. The plate shows the surface and the finish. The rule gives the number. Neither is asked to do the other's job.

Then four passes. The front laid flat. A scale pass with a known object beside the piece. A detail of whatever is most worth seeing close. And the label. The label pass matters more than people expect, because a woven label can date a garment to within a few years, and because it is the one piece of evidence in the file that we did not create ourselves.

The shadow problem, and why we stopped fixing it

Raking light is unkind by design, and anything lifted off the plate throws a long soft shadow across the cloth beside it. A collar does it. A patch pocket does it. A rolled cuff does it twice. Our first instinct was to lift those shadows in post. We did that for about two months.

Then a customer wrote to ask whether the images were retouched, because the edges looked too clean. It was a fair question and the honest answer was yes, slightly, and that we should stop. So the shadows stay. They are the signature of the method: if you can see the lamp falling across the plate, you know the file has not been worked on. Every image on this site carries it.

The shadow turns out to be information rather than a defect. Its length tells you how thick the cloth is. A collar that throws a deep shadow has body. A lapel that throws almost none has been pressed flat by forty years of hanging.

Where the method fails

It is not a universal answer and we would rather say so. Three things do not plate well.

Colour is the first. A single hard light source renders certain dyes badly, particularly saturated reds and some indigos. We set white balance against a grey card and correct nothing else, and where a colour still reads wrong we say so in the record in plain words. If a piece has a colour we cannot reproduce faithfully, the listing says do not trust this image for colour, and we will send additional photographs on request.

Drape is the second. A plate cannot show you how a wool crepe coat moves, because plating is the deliberate removal of movement. For pieces where drape is the whole point, a swing coat or a bias-cut skirt, we add a written description of how the cloth behaves in the hand. It is not as good as seeing it. We do not pretend it is.

Scale on very large garments is the third. A long overcoat needs three passes and the joins between them are visible if you look. We do not composite them into one seamless image, because compositing is where retouching begins. Three plates, three captions, no illusion of a single continuous view.

How to read a plate before you buy

If you are looking at one of our records, four things deserve your attention.

Read the crease pattern first. Fold lines running in straight parallels mean the piece was stored folded, usually in a drawer or a chest, which is a good sign for wool. Creases radiating from one point mean it was crumpled, which usually means it was stored badly, and it is worth checking the underarms.

Then look for fade differentials. A cotton jacket faded evenly all over was washed a lot. One faded at the shoulders and cuffs but not the body was worn outdoors a lot. Neither is a problem. They are different histories, and they tell you what to expect from the next decade of wear.

Third, look at the repair passes. Every repair we make is photographed. A neat darn in matched yarn on an elbow is a garment somebody cared about, and it will keep going. A repair we would not make ourselves means we turned the piece down.

Fourth, read the label pass against the size we assigned. If the label says medium and the register says small, that gap is telling you which decade the garment comes from. It is also telling you that the size on the listing was arrived at with a tape rather than copied off a tag.

That is the whole method. It is not complicated and there is nothing proprietary in it. If you sell secondhand clothing and you want to work this way, a copy stand and one hard lamp cost less than a decent portrait lens, and they will make your listings substantially more honest.

A model shows you a garment at its best hour. A plate shows you every hour it has already had.

What follows from it

Nothing is retouched out

Every consequence of this method is written into the record rather than corrected in a file. The shadow stays. The join between passes stays. The colour warning stays where a dye defeats the lamp. If a plate misleads you, that is a fault we want to hear about, because the plate is the only thing standing between a listing and a guess.

The same rule governs the bench. What we mend is photographed, priced into the piece and written into the record, and the nine months of figures behind that policy are set out in full.
