Leather, brass and canvas

Nine belts, bags, caps and small leather goods. Every dimension is in inches, every material is named, and every piece of hardware has been worked by hand before listing.

Measured in inches Conditioned by hand Free US shipping over $125

Accessories are where buying secondhand makes the plainest sense. A belt cut from full-grain bridle leather in 1994 is a better object than almost anything sold new at the same price today, because the leather was thicker and the edge was burnished rather than painted. Canvas bags, cotton caps and solid-brass buckles follow the same rule. These things were built with an expectation of decades.

The catch is that leather ages unevenly. A strap can look perfect on the grain side and be cracked through on the flesh side, where nobody looks. So every leather piece is flexed to its limit, the grain is read under a lens, and the stitching is pressed to find out whether the thread has gone brittle.

Belts are measured the way they should be, from the base of the buckle to the middle hole rather than end to end. That number is the size on the record. A belt listed as a 34 has its middle hole at 34 inches, with roughly an inch of adjustment either side.

Leather is conditioned with a neutral cream before photography and the date is written into the record. We do not dye, we do not paint edges to cover wear, and we do not polish out a scar. A scar is provenance. A crack is a defect, and defects are stated in the condition line in plain words.

Leather and hardware

What we do to a hide

Leather and metal are the two things on this page that can be quietly faked. Here is exactly what happens to each piece between the box it arrives in and the plate it is photographed on.

Conditioned by hand

Every leather piece is conditioned with a neutral cream before photography, and the month of that work is written into the record. Nothing is dyed. No edge is painted to cover wear. A scar is left where it is, because a scar is provenance, and a crack is called a defect in the condition line.

Hardware checked for movement

Buckles, D-rings, clutch backs and zips are worked by hand before a piece is listed. Each one has to turn freely, with no loose rivet behind it. Where a buckle is solid brass or solid nickel we say so, and we say when a fitting is plated rather than solid.

Flexed, read, pressed

A strap can look perfect on the grain and be cracked through on the flesh side. So every leather piece is flexed to its limit, the grain is read under a lens, and the stitching is pressed to find brittle thread. Repairs are saddle stitched in waxed linen and photographed.

Fit

Buying a belt that fits

  • Measure your current belt

    From the base of the buckle to the hole you actually use. That number is your belt size, and it is usually two inches larger than your trouser waist.

  • Check the width against your loops

    Most jeans take 1.5 in. Most tailored trousers take 1.25 in. A belt a quarter inch too wide will not pass the keeper.

  • Read the flesh side, not the grain

    The underside tells you whether a belt is one solid piece or two thin pieces glued together. Every belt we list has that side photographed.

  • Remember it can be altered

    A belt that is too long can be shortened at the buckle end by any cobbler. One that is too short cannot be lengthened. When in doubt, size up.
