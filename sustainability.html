Materials

Better than new. Still not a solution.

The claim, and its size

The honest version

Almost all of the clothing industry's environmental footprint sits in production: growing or synthesising fibre, spinning, dyeing, finishing, cutting, sewing and shipping a new garment into existence. A secondhand business does none of that. Every piece we sell already exists, and its production footprint was paid decades ago by somebody else.

That advantage is real, and it is also the beginning and the end of the environmental case for what we do. Reselling used clothing does not remove a garment from the waste stream permanently, it delays its arrival. It does not reduce anyone else's production. It does not make the textile recycling problem smaller. At best it keeps a well-made object in use for another ten or twenty years and displaces one new purchase somebody would otherwise have made.

We will not dress that up. There are no carbon offset certificates on this page, no percentage-of-profits pledge and no claim that buying a coat here saves a stated number of litres of water. Figures like that are usually industry averages applied to a garment nobody measured, and we will not publish numbers we cannot stand behind.

What we can do is describe the practice precisely enough for you to judge it yourself. That is the rest of this page: how we source, what we refuse, how we clean, what we repair, what happens to the pieces that fail, and what the packaging is made of.

Fibre

Materials policy

We buy fibres that repair well and last. Less a moral position than a practical one: a garment that cannot be mended has a short second life.

What we look for

  • Wool in all its forms. Shetland, lambswool, merino, Aran, Melton, tweed and worsted. It is repairable, warm when damp, naturally odour resistant and it takes a darn invisibly.
  • Cotton in heavy weights. Duck, denim, canvas, gabardine, corduroy and oxford cloth. Fades rather than fails, and patches cleanly.
  • Linen and linen blends, particularly in shirting and jackets.
  • Vegetable-tanned leather in belts, bags and small goods. Conditions, patinates and can be restitched.
  • Silk in scarves and linings, where a hand wash keeps it going for decades.
  • Wool and nylon blends at the cuffs and hems of sportswear, where the nylon is doing genuine structural work.

What we turn down

  • Bonded and coated synthetics where a polyurethane layer is peeling. There is no repair for delamination.
  • Fast-fashion knitwear in short-staple acrylic. It pills within one season and cannot be de-pilled without thinning the fabric.
  • Anything with a broken invisible zip in a lining that cannot be opened without destroying the garment.
  • Heavily felted wool that has been machine washed. Felting is irreversible and the piece will never fit its stated size again.
  • Garments with unidentifiable fibre content that fail a burn test on a loose thread. If we cannot name the fibre we cannot write care instructions.
  • Anything with a persistent odour after two cleaning attempts. Smoke and mildew do not always come out, and nobody wants a package that smells.

Before the plate

Cleaning

Every garment is cleaned before it is photographed, by the gentlest method the fibre will tolerate.

  • Wool knitwear is hand washed in cool water with a wool-specific detergent and dried flat in the studio. Never machine washed, whatever the label says.
  • Cotton outerwear and shirting is machine washed cold and line dried.
  • Tailored wool goes to a wet cleaner in Portland that uses water and biodegradable detergent rather than perchloroethylene. It costs us more per piece and produces a better result on old cloth.
  • Waxed cotton is never washed. It is sponged, stripped where the finish has failed, and rewaxed by hand.
  • Leather is wiped and conditioned with a neutral cream. No dye, no edge paint, no silicone.
  • Silk is hand washed and pressed damp.

Cleaning method and date appear on every product record. If a piece went through solvent cleaning, the record says so.

At the bench

Repair

Repair decides whether a garment survives. The following happens in house, or with a seamstress in Milwaukie.

  • Darning on knitwear in matched yarn, worked from the inside where possible.
  • Button replacement with matched vintage stock. We keep roughly two thousand loose buttons sorted by size and material.
  • Seam restitching where the original thread has gone brittle, in a thread weight that matches the original.
  • Lining replacement when the shell is sound but the lining has shattered. Usually cupro or silk habotai.
  • Saddle stitching on leather straps that have failed, in waxed linen thread.
  • Rewaxing on waxed cotton, by hand, with a wax bar and a heat gun.

Every repair is photographed and named in the condition line. A visible darn is not a defect. It is the reason the sweater still exists.

Waste

Where the rejects go

Roughly five of every six garments that come through the door are never listed. Throwing them away would make the whole exercise pointless.

  • Sound but not for us

    Wearable pieces that do not fit the register go to two local reuse organisations in the Portland metro area, sorted by size and season rather than dumped as a mixed bag.

  • Damaged, with usable cloth

    Anything holding a yard or more of sound fabric goes into the Re-cut salvage bin and becomes a vest, cap, mittens or an overshirt.

  • Hardware and trims

    Buttons, buckles, zips and D-rings are cut off and sorted into the parts drawers. Most of our button replacements come from here.

  • Genuinely finished

    Whatever remains goes to a textile recycler for fibre reclamation. It is the smallest stream and we would like it to be smaller.

Packaging

Orders ship in a plain kraft box or a paper mailer, both unbleached and both recyclable in ordinary US curbside programmes. Inside, garments are wrapped in uncoated tissue and secured with paper tape. There is no plastic polybag, no printed plastic sticker and no branded filler.

The one exception is the shipping label, a thermal label with a plastic face, because that is what carrier systems require. We have not found a workable alternative and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

Each order includes a printed record card for the piece, on uncoated card, and nothing else. No flyer, no discount insert, no sample sachet.

Shipping footprint

Everything ships ground within the United States by default. Expedited air shipping is available at checkout because people sometimes genuinely need it, but it is never the preselected option and it is never free.

Open questions

What we have not solved

Water use in cleaning

Wet cleaning uses less harmful chemistry than solvent cleaning, but it still uses water and energy. We have not measured our consumption per garment and we are not going to guess at a figure.

Returns freight

Every return is a second journey. We keep the rate low by publishing detailed measurements rather than by making returns difficult, but the emissions are real and they are ours.

The end of the line

Fibre reclamation for mixed and blended textiles remains poor at commercial scale in the United States. The pieces we send there are not being made into new clothing.

What ends up on the record

Written down, not implied

Cleaning method and date go on every product record. Solvent cleaning is stated plainly when it happened. Every repair is photographed and named in the condition line, which is why a darn or a replaced button is visible on the plate rather than tidied out of the picture. Nothing here is a pledge about the future. It is a description of what already happens to each garment.

If you work in textile reclamation or wet cleaning and think we have something wrong, we would genuinely like to hear it. Write to us.
