The register, divided

Three shelves, nothing else

The wardrobe stays small on purpose. Everything we buy has to fall into one of these three, and it has to still be worth wearing a decade from now.

Outerwear

Chore coats, field jackets, Melton overcoats and truckers. Shell fabric and cloth weight are listed on every single piece.

See outerwear

Knitwear

Shetland, lambswool, merino and cotton. Every sweater goes under the lamp for moth damage and pilling before it earns a plate number.

See knitwear

Accessories

Belts, bags, caps and scarves. Leather is conditioned by hand and every piece of hardware is worked to check it still moves.

See accessories
The method

Flat, lit from the side, measured twice

A model wearing a coat tells you how it fits that model. A garment laid flat under raking light tells you what you are actually buying.

So nothing here is worn for the camera. Each piece is laid on the plate, lit from one side so the weave and the wear both throw a shadow, and measured edge to edge with a steel tape. The crease stays. The fade line stays. The mended cuff is photographed at the same scale as the sleeve it belongs to, because that is the honest way round.

How the wardrobe refills

A standing sixty, plus the first Friday

Roughly sixty pieces stay listed the year round. They are the things that work in March and again in October: chore coats, crewnecks, straight-leg denim, a belt that will outlive the jeans. That part of the register is never on a season clock.

Everything else arrives as a dated drop. We buy in bulk from estate clear-outs, rag houses and a handful of long-term sellers, then spend three weeks washing, mending, measuring and plating. The finished batch goes live at 11:00 a.m. Pacific Time on the first Friday of the month.
