Measurements, not labels

The numbers we sell on

Every size on this site is assigned after the garment is measured flat, in inches, against a steel rule. We do not trust the label and neither should you.

Measured flat, in inches Four or five points per piece Label plated, never used to size One garment. One size. One owner next.

A wool sweater knitted in Scotland in 1979 and marked medium will usually measure smaller across the chest than a sweater marked small from a mainstream American brand today. Neither maker made a mistake. Sizing conventions have drifted upward for fifty years, differently in every country and every category, and anyone selling vintage clothing who quotes you the original label is handing that problem to you.

So the size in every listing is our size, worked out from four or five measurements taken with the garment pressed flat and closed. The original label is plated separately so you can read what it says, but it is not what we sell on.

The most reliable way to buy secondhand clothing online is to measure something you already own and wear, then set those numbers beside ours. Body measurements are useful for tailoring. They are close to useless for judging whether a 1970s coat cut for a suit underneath will sit right over a sweater.

Below are the ranges we use for each category, the exact points we take, and a short method for measuring your own garments. If any of it reads unclear, send us the numbers and we will tell you honestly whether a piece will work. We would rather answer an email than process a return.

Denim and trousers

Waist is measured across the top of the waistband, flat, then doubled. Vintage denim marked 32 frequently measures 30 or 34 depending on the decade and the wash, so we quote our own measurement first and the label second.

Rigid denim will stretch roughly half an inch to a full inch at the waist within the first month of wear and then hold. Sanforized denim will not shrink further. Unsanforized denim will, and any listing for it says so in bold on the record.

The method

How we measure

The garment is closed, laid flat on the table and smoothed out, and the tape runs edge to edge across the piece itself. Nothing is stretched, nothing hangs over an edge, and nothing is taken off a body. What goes onto the record card is the flat number.

Where a figure has to be doubled to give a circumference, the page says so. The waistband is measured across the top, flat, then doubled. A flat chest can be doubled the same way if you want the full round, but every table above prints the flat figure.

Measure what already fits

A tape measure, a flat surface and five minutes. Do not measure your body. Measure the thing that fits you.

  • Lay it flat and close it

    Button or zip the garment, lay it face up on a table or the floor, and smooth it out. Do not stretch it and do not let it drape over an edge.

  • Chest, one inch below the armhole

    Measure straight across from edge to edge. That is the flat chest. Double it if you want the circumference, but our tables use the flat number.

  • Sleeve from the centre back

    Start at the middle of the collar seam at the back of the neck, run the tape over the shoulder point and down to the end of the cuff.

  • Length from the collar base

    Start where the collar meets the body at the back and measure straight down to the hem. Do not include the collar itself.

  • Write the numbers down

    Keep them in your phone. Once you hold the flat measurements of two or three garments that fit you well, buying vintage online stops being a gamble.

Before you buy

Ask before you buy

Send us the flat measurements of a garment that fits you and the register number you are weighing up. We will tell you honestly whether it will work, including when the answer is no.

We have talked people out of pieces more than once, usually because a coat cut for a suit underneath will swim on somebody who wants it over a t-shirt. That is a better outcome for everybody than a return, and it costs us nothing but an email.
