Repair

Nine months of mending, written down

By Devin Aristide Falk · July 30, 2026 · 10 minute read

Every garment that reaches the bench gets a line in a paper ledger: what was wrong with it, what we did, how long it took, and whether we would do it again. Nine months of those lines have hardened into something close to a policy, and the policy is colder than we expected it to be.

The short version. About a third of the pieces we buy need repair before they can be listed. We finish roughly seventy percent of those. The rest are broken down for parts or passed on, not because they cannot be mended but because mending them would cost more in bench time than the finished garment could ever return. Writing that arithmetic down is uncomfortable. It is also the most useful thing we have learned.

Six repairs always worth doing

Buttons. A missing button is four to twelve minutes depending on whether it is a shank, a four-hole or a covered button, and it turns an unsellable garment into a sellable one. We hold roughly two thousand loose buttons sorted into drawers by material and diameter, most of them taken off garments that were past saving. Matching is done by eye in daylight and the match is almost never perfect. The replacement is named in the condition line and photographed, because a buyer who knows about it will not be surprised by it.

Seam restitching. Thread goes brittle long before cloth does, and mid-century cotton thread goes first. A side seam that has opened three inches is fifteen minutes at the machine, and the garment is sound again for another twenty years. The rule is to match the original thread weight rather than reach for something stronger.

Darning knitwear. A moth hole in a Shetland is twenty to forty minutes of hand work in matched yarn, worked from the inside so the face stays smooth. It is the best job in the workroom and it rescues pieces that would otherwise be worth nothing. Yarn comes from the seam allowance of the same garment where there is any to spare, which gives a perfect match, and from the yarn box where there is not.

Lining replacement. Old linings shatter. Acetate and rayon from the 1950s onward go brittle and split at the shoulder and under the arms while the wool shell is still entirely sound. A full body lining is two to three hours and costs us more than any other routine job, but it is the difference between a coat that is finished and a coat with another thirty years in it. We use cupro or silk habotai, and we say which.

Rewaxing. Waxed cotton loses its finish in patches. Stripping the old wax with a heat gun and a cloth, then laying on bar wax by hand, is about ninety minutes for a field jacket. Done properly it restores real water repellency rather than the look of it, and the first rain makes the difference obvious.

Saddle stitching leather. A strap that has failed at the stitch line can be resewn by hand in waxed linen thread in about forty minutes. The new stitch sits a shade lighter than the old one for the first year and then it matches. Putting a machine through old leather is a mistake, because the machine perforates a line that becomes the next failure.

A repair thread stronger than the cloth will tear the cloth next time instead of breaking itself. Match the original weight. Always.

Four we decline

Reweaving. Invisible reweaving on a hole in worsted wool is remarkable work and a specialist charges between sixty and a hundred and forty dollars per hole. On a coat we would sell at two hundred, the sum does not close. On a coat we would sell at eight hundred it might, but we do not deal in eight hundred dollar coats. Those pieces go on to sellers who do.

Delamination. A bonded or coated fabric whose polyurethane layer has started to peel cannot be repaired at all. It is a chemical failure, not a stitching one, and the peel will travel across the whole panel. We turn these down at intake and say why.

Felted wool. A wool sweater that has been through a warm machine wash has shrunk for good. The fibres have locked together and no amount of blocking, conditioning or pulling will release them. A hair conditioner soak and a blocking board can sometimes win half an inch. They will not win two, and a piece that no longer matches any size we would assign is a piece we cannot list honestly.

Zip replacement on a lined garment. A broken zip in an unlined jacket is forty minutes. In a fully lined coat with a bagged-out front edge it is three to four hours, because the lining has to come apart and go back together around the new tape. We will do it for a piece we think is exceptional. Usually we do not, and the honest reason is the labour.

The arithmetic, plainly

Bench time is costed at a real hourly rate, because pretending your own labour is free is how a small business goes quietly bust. A repair is worth doing when the realistic sale price of the finished garment clears the intake cost, the cleaning cost, the repair labour and a margin that keeps the lights on.

In practice that gives one simple test. Under an hour of work is almost always worth doing on any garment we would have bought in the first place. Between one and three hours it depends entirely on the piece: a new lining in a good Melton overcoat clears the bar easily, the same job in a mid-weight raincoat does not. Above three hours the answer is no, unless the garment is genuinely unusual.

That test also explains a question we get about pricing. Two similar coats can sit forty dollars apart because one took ninety minutes of work and the other took ten. We are not pricing the label or the decade. We are pricing what it took to bring the thing to a condition we would wear ourselves.

We do not price the decade or the label. We price the hours it took to make the garment worth owning again.

Nine months in numbers

Between November 2025 and July 2026 the ledger records 412 repair jobs across 288 garments. Buttons are the largest single category at 131 jobs, then seam restitching at 96 and darning at 74. Lining replacement appears 18 times, rewaxing 22, leather stitching 31. The remaining 40 are miscellaneous: hem re-turns, belt-loop rebuilds, cuff facings, a handful of pocket bags.

Refusals are harder to count, because many happen at the sorting table before a piece is ever logged. Of the garments that were logged and then abandoned, 34 were felted knitwear, 19 were delamination, 11 were zip replacements we decided against, and 6 were holes that needed professional reweaving.

None of those figures are impressive and none are meant to be. They are here because people ask what "repaired where needed" means on a product page, and a number answers that better than an adjective.

What this means if you are buying

Three practical things follow.

First, a visible repair is usually a good sign. A garment somebody bothered to darn in 1988 was a garment worth darning, and it reached you partly because of that. The pieces in the worst condition are almost always the ones nobody ever mended.

Second, learn the two repairs that will keep almost anything alive: sewing on a button properly, and darning a small hole in knitwear. Both take an evening to learn from a video and neither needs more than a needle, thread and a darning mushroom. Between them they cover most of the failures that send clothes to landfill.

Third, buy fibres that repair well. Wool, heavy cotton, linen and vegetable-tanned leather can all be mended by hand with ordinary tools. Bonded synthetics, thin acrylic knits and glued leather cannot be mended at all, at any price, by anyone. That distinction decides how long a garment lasts more than almost anything else printed on its label.

The line under it

Every repair is on the record

Nothing above stays in the ledger. Each mend is photographed on the plate, named in the condition line and carried into the price, so the work is visible before you buy rather than discovered afterwards. When we decline a piece we say which of the four reasons applied.

A garment that has been mended once has already proved it is worth mending again. That is the argument for keeping a small wardrobe on a schedule instead of replacing it.
