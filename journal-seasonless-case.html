Wearing

A seasonless wardrobe is a schedule, not a palette

By Ingrid Sawyer-Baptiste · June 12, 2026 · 9 minute read

The seasonless wardrobe is usually sold as a matter of taste. Choose neutral colours, buy classic shapes, avoid trends. None of that is wrong, but it describes the easy part of the problem and skips the part that decides whether the thing actually works, which is upkeep.

I spent four years building what I thought was a seasonless wardrobe and watching it come apart every eighteen months. The colours were right. The shapes were right. What was wrong is that I treated sixteen garments as objects I owned rather than as a set of maintenance requirements, several of which contradict each other.

16 pieces 4 fabric families February to November 4 hours of upkeep a year

The actual sixteen

Here is what I wear from February through November in Oregon, which means anything from about thirty degrees to about ninety, sometimes inside the same week, and rain over most of it.

Four knits: a Shetland crewneck, a fine merino turtleneck, a cotton boatneck and a heavy shawl cardigan. Three shirts: an oxford button-down, a chambray work shirt and a linen camp shirt. Three trousers: straight-leg denim, a cotton chino and a wool flannel for cold months. Two jackets: a cotton chore coat and a waxed field jacket. One Melton overcoat. One belt. One pair of boots. One canvas tote.

That is the list, and none of it is remarkable. The Shetland and the boatneck do most of the work. The overcoat comes out perhaps thirty days a year and is by a wide margin the most expensive thing on the list, which is a trade I would make again, because those thirty days are the ones where being cold matters.

What holds the set together is not that the colours agree, although they do. It is that every piece has a defined maintenance interval, and the intervals are staggered, so nothing is ever out of action at the same time as the thing that would cover for it.

Four fabric families, four demands

Wool knitwear wants air, not water. Wool resists odour because the fibre traps and neutralises the compounds that cause it, so a sweater worn all day recovers overnight on a hanger by an open window. My four knits are washed three or four times a year each, by hand, in cool water, dried flat. Between washes they are aired after every wear and combed once a month at the underarms and the front, where a coat rubs.

Woven cotton is the opposite. Shirts go in after one or two wears, because cotton holds everything. Cold wash, gentle cycle, line dry, iron damp if you want the collar sharp. The cost of cotton shirting is high frequency and low effort, which is the easiest kind of upkeep to keep up with.

Tailored wool wants brushing and steam and almost never wants cleaning. A wool flannel trouser is brushed after every wear with a stiff clothes brush, which lifts surface soil before it works into the fibre, and steamed once a month to drop creases. It sees a cleaner once a season at most. Solvent and heat are what age tailored wool. Wearing it is not.

Waxed cotton and leather want an appointment rather than a routine. The field jacket is rewaxed every eighteen months or so, judged by whether water still beads on the shoulders. The belt and the boots are conditioned twice a year, in spring and autumn, on the same weekend. Doing both on one day turns it into a habit instead of a decision.

Wool wants air. Cotton wants water. Tailored wool wants a brush. Waxed cotton wants a date in the calendar. Confuse those four and pieces start failing in the second year.

The schedule that makes it work

Written out, the whole thing is about four hours a year on top of the laundry that would happen anyway.

After every wear. Knits go on a hanger by a window overnight, then get folded. Never hung long term, because the shoulders stretch. Tailored trousers are brushed and hung by the cuffs. Everything else goes in the basket or back in the drawer.

Weekly. Shirts wash with the ordinary load, cold, and go on the line. Denim does not. Denim washed weekly is denim that will fade out in eighteen months. Mine goes in roughly every six weeks unless something happens to it.

Monthly. Twenty minutes with a knit comb across all four sweaters, working the underarms and the front. This is the highest-return habit on the whole list. Pilling is not damage in itself, but pills left to grow abrade the yarn around them, and abraded yarn becomes a thin patch, and a thin patch becomes a hole.

Seasonally. Two dates in the calendar, the first weekend of April and the first weekend of October. Hand wash all the knits and dry them flat. Condition the belt and the boots. Check every garment for loose buttons and sew them before they go. Look at the underarms of everything wool under a strong lamp for the first signs of moth.

Annually, or thereabouts. Rewax the field jacket when the water stops beading. Take the overcoat to the wet cleaner at the end of winter rather than the start, so it goes into storage clean. Moths are drawn to soiled wool, not clean wool, and storing a coat dirty for six months is how you lose it.

What I got wrong for four years

Three mistakes, all of them ordinary.

I washed knitwear far too often, on the theory that clean is better. What actually happens is that every wash, however careful, agitates the fibre slightly and takes life off the garment. A sweater that is aired properly does not need washing after three wears. Mine now go eight or ten.

I stored wool in plastic. Vacuum bags and sealed bins feel protective and do the opposite. Wool needs moving air, and sealed plastic traps moisture, which invites mildew and creates exactly the humid conditions moth larvae prefer. A cotton garment bag and a cedar block work better and cost less.

I bought replacements instead of repairs. When the Shetland holed at the elbow I bought another Shetland, because a new sweater felt like a solution and a darning needle felt like a chore. The darning takes half an hour and leaves a small neat patch nobody has ever remarked on. Four years of that habit is the difference between a wardrobe of sixteen pieces and a wardrobe of forty, most of it unworn.

Why old clothes suit this best

A garment made in 1985 has already survived forty years, which says something firm about how it was built. It has done its shrinking. Its dye has settled. Any structural weakness has already declared itself. In engineering terms it is past the early-failure part of the curve.

It also means the upkeep is legible. An old Shetland smells faintly of lanolin when damp and has a slightly hairy surface, and both of those tell you it is woollen spun and wants hand washing. A modern superwash merino has been treated to survive a machine and feels entirely different in the hand. Knowing which one you are holding decides how you look after it, and old garments are usually easier to read.

The last thing is scale. Sixteen pieces is a number a person can actually maintain. Forty is not, which is why the fortieth garment in most wardrobes has been worn twice. A seasonless wardrobe has to stay small enough that the schedule above fits into four hours a year, because a schedule nobody keeps is not a schedule.

Sixteen pieces is a number you can keep. Forty is a number that keeps you.

Where to start

Build it one interval at a time

Nobody assembles sixteen right pieces in a season, and trying to is how the forty-garment wardrobe happens. Take one fabric family at a time, learn what it asks for, and add the next piece only when the last one has been through a full cycle of its own schedule.

Every listing in the register carries the measurements, the condition notes and the plates you need to judge whether a piece will earn its interval. Two dates in the calendar do the rest.
