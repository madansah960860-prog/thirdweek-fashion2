Nine plates · Knitwear

Wool, held to the light

Nine sweaters and cardigans, each a single unit. Fibre, gauge, body length and sleeve length sit on every record, along with what the knit has already lived through.

Gauge counted on every plate Hand washed, dried flat 30-day returns

Old knitwear is the best value in secondhand clothing and the easiest thing to buy badly. A wool sweater knitted in 1978 was almost certainly spun from longer, stronger fibre than an equivalent piece made today, which is why so many of them are still sound after four decades. It is also why the ones that were washed wrong are beyond help. Felted wool cannot be un-felted, and no amount of steaming brings a shrunken chest back.

So the first thing we do with a knit is measure it, then check the gauge by counting stitches over two inches to see how tightly it was made. A five-gauge Aran and a fourteen-gauge merino behave differently on the body, in the wash and across years of wear. Both numbers go into the record.

Then it goes under a strong lamp. We read along every seam, inside both cuffs, under the arms and around the neckband, looking for moth grazing, which shows as thin patches rather than clean holes. Anything we find is darned on the bench in matched yarn or written into the condition line. Repairs are photographed rather than hidden. A well darned elbow is not a flaw. It is the reason the sweater still exists.

Every piece here has been washed by hand in cool water with a wool-safe detergent, dried flat on a rack, and pressed only where the seams asked for it. None of them have been machine washed by us, whatever the label claims.

Under the lamp

What we check under the lamp

Every knit is read under a strong lamp before it is photographed. Three faults decide whether a sweater is listed, darned or turned away, and all three are written into the record.

Moth grazing

Moth damage rarely looks like a clean hole. It shows as a thin patch where the surface has been grazed away, usually inside a cuff, under an arm or around the neckband. What we find is either darned on the bench in matched yarn or stated in the condition line. Repairs are photographed, never hidden.

Pilling and surface

Pilling gathers first at the underarms and wherever a coat collar rubs. Light pilling is noted on the record rather than shaved off, because a razor thins the knit. Where a piece has a brushed surface, such as alpaca, we check the halo for bald patches instead.

Seams, ribs and gauge

We read along every seam, then test whether the cuffs and waistband still spring back. A rib that has lost its memory is stated. Gauge is counted over two inches so you know whether you are buying a five-gauge Aran or a twelve-gauge merino before it arrives.

Care

Washing old wool

  • Cool water, no movement

    Felting comes from heat plus agitation, not from water. Fill the basin first, then lower the sweater in and leave it alone for ten minutes.

  • Squeeze, never wring

    Press the water out against the side of the basin, then roll the piece in a dry towel and stand on it. That removes more water than any spin cycle you should be using.

  • Dry flat, reshape damp

    Lay it on a rack and push it back to the measurements in its record while it is still wet. Wool sets in whatever shape it dries in.

  • Wash less than you think

    Wool is self-cleaning to a degree that cotton is not. Airing a sweater overnight handles most wears. Three or four washes a year is normal.
